# ADR-015: A layered glossary — curated wins, reference corpora are imported not scraped

## Context
The product's screens are dense with vocabulary that means something specific and contested:
`verdict`, `commitment`, `UCO`, and `artefact type` are ours; `least privilege`, `PHI`,
`cardholder data` and `SoA` belong to laws and standards. A user reading a gap has no way to
check what a word means without leaving the product.

The obvious reference is [compliancedictionary.com](https://compliancedictionary.com) (Unified
Compliance / Network Frontiers), which carries ~377k terms. Probing it settled that it is not
an option to build on: definitions come from `GET /term/<term>/relationships`, an undocumented
internal AngularJS endpoint that returns `403 {"message":"search limit exceeded"}` after three
anonymous calls, with `/search/controls` and `/search/citations` returning empty arrays without
a subscription. Their content is licensed commercially — that *is* the business. Their scale is
also partly an artefact of counting: only ~4k of their entries are terms unique to a document;
the rest are term-instances across ~1,000 mapped authority documents.

The first cut of this feature was 69 hand-written entries. That was honest about provenance but
had a real weakness: the entries citing NIST and legislation were written *from recollection of*
those documents, not from their text. A glossary whose value proposition is attributability
cannot rest on that.

## Decision
**Three layers in one lookup, with a fixed precedence.**

1. **Curated** — `app/content/glossary.yaml`, ~70 entries, hand-written. Holds the platform
   vocabulary nobody else defines, the legal definitions (GDPR Art. 4, HIPAA §160.103), and
   deliberately *paraphrased* summaries of copyrighted standards (ISO, PCI SSC, AICPA).
2. **NIST CSRC** — `app/content/glossary-nist.json`, ~3.9k entries, generated by
   `scripts/import_nist_glossary.py` from NIST's own published bulk export.
3. **Trustworthy AI** — `app/content/glossary-ai.json`, 447 entries, generated by
   `scripts/import_ai_glossary.py` from NIST's "The Language of Trustworthy AI" CSV.

**Why the AI layer ranks last, and why it is a different kind of source.** NIST *wrote* the
CSRC glossary, so that text is US Government work. The AI glossary is a *compilation of
quotations*: of its 447 terms only ~107 carry a definition from a public-domain source, and
the rest quote ISO/IEC TS 5723, IEEE vocabularies, Russell & Norvig and individual papers.
Every entry therefore keeps the citation of whoever actually wrote it, `source` names that
publisher rather than NIST, and where CSRC and the AI file define the same word
("accountability") CSRC wins. Importing all 447 verbatim was an explicit product decision
taken with the copyright position understood, not a default.

**Its "Related terms and synonyms" column is not aliases.** It lists *related* terms:
"active learning agent" points at "passive learning agent", its opposite, and "biometric data"
at "personal data". Importing that column as aliases would resolve one word to another word's
definition. Acronyms are parsed out of the term text instead ("human-computer interaction
(HCI)"), which is safe, and ambiguous ones are dropped as everywhere else.

**This is a download, not a scrape.** NIST publishes the whole glossary as
`glossary-export.zip`, refreshed daily, alongside a `.meta` file carrying its size and sha256;
the importer verifies that checksum and refuses a mismatch. NIST publications are US Government
work and therefore public domain, so definitions are reproduced verbatim with their source
publication and DOI intact. Nothing in the running app touches the network — the import runs
occasionally and its output is committed.

**NIST's bulk export is not NIST's glossary, and the curated layer is what covers the
difference.** The export carries 9,140 records, but the bare terms `confidentiality`,
`threat`, `vulnerability`, `authentication` and `least privilege` are absent from it entirely
— all five are on csrc.nist.gov with multiple definitions. Only compound forms
("Confidentiality Impact", "insider threat") made the export. So the import does *not* make the
hand-written entries redundant for core security vocabulary; for exactly those words the
curated layer is the only source, which raises the bar on their accuracy rather than lowering
it. Anyone tempted to delete a curated entry "because NIST covers it" should check the corpus
first.

**A cited definition carries only the cited source's words.** `Term.note` exists because
several curated entries had grown a sentence of our own commentary inside a definition
attributed to NIST — "Audit log" quoted SP 800-53 and then added our point about
tamper-evidence, with nothing marking where NIST stopped. Definition is now the source's text;
our gloss goes in `note` and renders visibly apart from it. Where an entry is our own summary
of a source rather than a quotation, `source` says "Adapted from …" so nothing reads as a
quotation that was never checked. Both are enforced by tests.

Verifying the NIST-cited entries against the actual publications found four real errors:
`Availability`, `Confidentiality` and `Integrity` were attributed to FIPS 199 when NIST
attributes that statutory wording (44 U.S.C. § 3542) to FIPS 200; `Authentication` was
attributed to SP 800-53 rather than FIPS 200; `Multi-factor authentication` cited the
superseded SP 800-63-3 for wording that is SP 800-172A's; and `Threat` and `Risk` were
paraphrases missing clauses of the SP 800-30 Rev. 1 text they claimed to quote.

**Curated wins every name collision.** NIST also defines `encryption`, `attestation` and
`availability`; ours are the ones written deliberately for this product and for the frameworks
it supports, so they survive. This mirrors the precedence a compliance lexicographer applies
(law > standard > our own wording), which the `source` field records on every entry.
`_merge_terms` takes the layers in precedence order rather than two named arguments, because
the third one proved the ordering is the actual rule and not a special case.

**A term's own name outranks being another entry's alias.** Merging in one pass silently
dropped ~357 defined NIST terms whose name happened to be the spelled-out form of some other
entry's acronym — "Additional Authenticated Data" is both a real term and the expansion of
`AAD`. Canonical names are therefore all claimed in a first pass, before any alias is
considered. Where an alias *is* already claimed, only the alias is dropped and the term is
kept: it stays findable under its own name, and the acronym keeps resolving to whoever owns it
instead of flipping to whichever file loaded last.

**Ambiguous acronyms resolve to nothing rather than to something arbitrary.** `CI` abbreviates
several unrelated NIST terms; the importer drops an alias claimed by more than one term. Exact
lookup then 404s and search still finds all of them — better than putting a confidently wrong
definition under the word a user clicked.

**Curated outranks imported in search, and is the whole default view.** With ~3.9k reference
entries against ~70 curated ones, listing everything buries the words the product actually
uses. An empty query returns the curated layer only; the corpus is reached by searching. The
same choice keeps the tooltip cache at ~20KB rather than 1.5MB.

**Content, not a table.** The glossary is identical for every organisation, so it lives with
`uco.yaml` and the framework packs and is loaded at import time — no `org_id`, no RLS, no
migration. `load()` gained `@lru_cache` because five modules call it at import and each was
re-parsing every content file; the imported layer is JSON rather than YAML because nothing
hand-edits it and `json.loads` is ~10x faster on 1.5MB.

## Alternatives considered
- **Licence the UCF API.** The only way to get their content lawfully and the right answer if
  in-product access to 377k terms is ever a requirement. Rejected for now: it is a procurement
  decision, and the free path covers the vocabulary this product actually uses.
- **Link out to compliancedictionary.com.** Costs one line and stays available as a fallback,
  but throws the user out of the product mid-task and integrates with nothing.
- **Scrape their definitions endpoint.** Rejected on every axis: rate-limited to three calls,
  undocumented and unstable, and it is their licensed content.
- **Import everything NIST publishes (all 9,140 records).** Rejected: 5,248 have
  `definitions: null` — they are acronym pointers with nothing to show in a tooltip. Only the
  3,892 with real definitions are imported.
- **Keep all definitions per term.** NIST carries up to 39 for `authenticate`, one per
  publication. The first is the primary; a glossary entry that dumps all 39 is unreadable.
- **A database table with a trigram index.** Premature at ~4k rows: search is a 4ms linear scan
  over names lowercased once at load, and exact lookup is an O(1) dict built in
  `Content.model_post_init`. Revisit if the corpus grows an order of magnitude or needs
  stemming rather than substring matching.

## Consequences
- `scripts/import_nist_glossary.py` must be re-run to pick up NIST's changes; the output is
  committed and the app never fetches at runtime. It is the only supported way to change
  `glossary-nist.json` — the file says so in its own `_comment`.
- The 23 curated entries citing NIST/FIPS have now been checked against their sources (10 by
  diff against the corpus, the rest against csrc.nist.gov), and four citation errors plus two
  incomplete quotations were corrected. The GDPR and HIPAA entries have *not* been verified
  this way — those regimes are not in NIST's corpus and would need checking against
  eur-lex/eCFR directly.
- `_check_terms` now guards only the hand-written file, where a duplicate is a typo and must
  fail loudly. Collisions involving the import are expected and resolved by `_merge_terms`.
- Fixed in passing, since all content reads route through one place: `Path.read_text()` was
  decoding content with the platform default (cp1252 on Windows), silently mangling every
  em-dash in every pack as `â€"`. Now explicitly UTF-8, with a regression test.
- `.hint` no longer inherits its host's typography, and `.meta-strip` no longer clips overflow —
  both were latent, and surfaced only when a tooltip was first anchored inside that strip.
